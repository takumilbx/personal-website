## Context and stakeholders

In August 2019 I shadowed a senior researcher from the Thailand Development Research Institute through fieldwork evaluating an ICT-in-education policy at a rural school, and it left me with a question: why does technology so often reach the classroom door and stop there? Four years later I made it my senior thesis at Thammasat University's School of Global Studies, advised by Dr. Shekh Mohammad Altafur Rahman: Bangkok's Digital Classroom policy against Japan's GIGA School Program, two one-device-per-student initiatives I could read in their own languages.

On the Bangkok side the stakeholders were the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration that runs the policy, the one BMA school where I collected field data, and its teachers, students and parents. On the Japanese side, a national programme and the schools it reached. That same summer I was in the Deputy Governor's office sorting Bangkok's 47 municipal education policies into 8 focus areas, so I saw the policy from the office and the classroom at once.

## The problem

Both governments had put a device in every student's hands. Neither could take for granted that the device would turn into a lesson. The thesis asked what stands between a rollout and a classroom practice.

## What I did

I ran it as a comparative case study across two terms in 2023 and 2024. For Bangkok I collected field data at one BMA school. For Japan, where I could not yet sit in a classroom, I built the case from policy documentation. Then I read the two against each other, looking for barriers that recurred in both rather than in one. The thesis was graded A in both terms.

## What the evidence showed

Three barriers recurred in both contexts: uneven teacher digital skills, slow connectivity, and parental concern about whether the devices actually helped learning. None of them is about the device. A programme that only counts devices per student cannot see any of them. So I concluded that device programmes must address students, teachers, parents and the learning environment together. ICT is not a silver bullet. That conclusion became the question I carried to the University of Tokyo, where my master's thesis now looks at how expert teachers in GIGA School classrooms decide to use, adapt or withhold the technology in front of them.
