## Context and stakeholders

In 2024, the year I finished at Thammasat, I was a Research Assistant for the United Nations ESCAP on a policy paper about older persons' labour force participation under digital transformation and the fourth industrial revolution. Until then almost everything I had studied was about children and classrooms. This was the first time I looked at the other end of the digital divide, at people asked to keep working in an economy that moved online faster than they did.

At the centre were older workers across the Asia-Pacific region. Around them were the governments of Japan, Korea and China running initiatives for older people's digital literacy and inclusion, the regional policymakers the paper was written for, and the report's lead consultant, who mentored me through it. The paper is credited to McFarlane, Mieruch and Ruedreal, and the last of those is me. Japan I already knew from the classroom side through my senior thesis. Korea and China were new ground.

## The problem

Digital transformation is rewriting what a job requires, and older people risk leaving the labour force not because they cannot work but because no one has shown them the tools the work now runs on. My part was to find the national initiatives in three East Asian countries and write each up for policymakers elsewhere in the region.

## What I did

I sourced and wrote the country case studies for Japan, Korea and China, each on a national initiative for older people's digital literacy and inclusion. Sourcing meant desk review and evidence synthesis: locating the initiative, checking what it claimed against what it documented, and writing it up in the form a regional policy paper needs. Digital literacy for older workers is lifelong learning in its most literal sense, and it sits on the same digital divide I had been studying in classrooms.

## What the evidence showed

The paper was published on 2 December 2024 in ESCAP's Social Development Policy Papers series as "Strengthening the labour force participation of older persons in the context of digital transformation and the fourth industrial revolution: insights from the Asia-Pacific region", with my three country cases inside it and my name as a contributor. The outcome I can point to is the artefact itself. What I cannot yet report is what the paper concluded region-wide.
